## Pre-match diagnostic

### Why does Model 2 predict a winner when the most likely score is 1-1?

All 10 GW4 fixtures had 1-1 as their most likely individual scoreline.  At the same time, Model 2 predicted a home or away win in every fixture.

This looks contradictory, but the two outputs answer different questions.

Take Crystal Palace v Ipswich as a practical example:

- Crystal Palace win: **43.9%**
- Draw: **24.5%**
- Ipswich win: **31.6%**
- Most likely individual scoreline: **1-1**

The 1-1 prediction means that, when every individual score such as 1-0, 2-0, 2-1, 0-0, 1-1 and 1-2 is considered separately, 1-1 has the highest individual probability.

The 43.9% Crystal Palace win probability is calculated differently.  It combines the probabilities of **every scoreline in which Crystal Palace win**, rather than relying on one individual score.

For example, 1-0, 2-0, 2-1, 3-0 and 3-1 may each individually be less likely than 1-1.  When all possible Crystal Palace winning scores are added together, however, they account for 43.9% of the overall probability.  This is higher than the combined draw probability of 24.5% and the combined Ipswich win probability of 31.6%.

Model 2 therefore selected Crystal Palace as the 1X2 prediction even though 1-1 was the single most likely exact score.

The calculation is internally consistent.  The more important diagnostic question is why **1-1 appears so frequently as the most likely scoreline**.

This was treated as a diagnostic hypothesis rather than a conclusion.  The GW4 results provide another prospective test of the behaviour.

## Draw and scoreline behaviour

GW4 strengthened two behaviours already visible in the first three Gameweeks.

Model 2 predicted **zero draws** from the 10 fixtures.  Four matches actually finished as draws:

- Liverpool 0-0 Fulham
- Bournemouth 2-2 Brentford
- Chelsea 2-2 Hull City
- Tottenham 0-0 Everton

All **10/10** fixtures had 1-1 as their modal predicted scoreline, yet **none** finished 1-1.

The distinction between these two diagnostics remains important.  The repeated 1-1 modal score does not itself cause the model to select a draw, because 1X2 probabilities aggregate all possible home-win, draw and away-win scorelines.  However, the combination of highly concentrated modal scores and an inability to select draws is now a repeatable live behaviour that warrants deeper investigation.

These observations do not yet prove the underlying cause.  They provide increasingly clear targets for diagnosis.

## What we learned

GW4 did not reverse the deterioration seen in GW3.  Model 2 again achieved only 30.0% 1X2 accuracy, taking cumulative live performance to 40.0% after 40 matches.

Three findings now deserve particular attention.

First, **draw discrimination is a persistent live weakness**.  Model 2 has assigned meaningful probability to draws, generally around the mid-20% range, but a draw has never become its highest-probability 1X2 selection.  With 14 actual draws in 40 matches, this behaviour is now material to live performance.

Second, **modal scoreline concentration remains extreme**.  The fact that 33/40 fixtures have produced the same 1-1 modal score suggests that the goal model may not be differentiating fixture scoring environments sufficiently.  The previously observed xG compression is therefore an important hypothesis to investigate, but the live results alone do not establish it as the cause.

Third, the model's poor live performance should not be reduced to a single headline accuracy number.  In GW4 it still beat all three simple baselines, and its probability metrics were better than the Historical Outcome Frequency baseline.  The diagnostic task is therefore to identify which components of Model 2 continue to add value and which components are limiting performance.

## Why Model 2 remains frozen through GW5

Model 2 remains the official production benchmark through GW5.

The purpose of the initial five-Gameweek period is to obtain **50 untouched prospective predictions** from the same frozen model.  Changing Model 2 after GW4 would contaminate that benchmark and make subsequent improvement harder to assess cleanly.

This does not mean model development needs to wait passively.

GW4 has sharpened the areas that should be investigated at the GW5 checkpoint:

1. draw discrimination and why draw probability never becomes the 1X2 argmax;
2. 1-1 modal-score concentration;
3. whether xG estimates are too compressed between materially different fixtures;
4. performance by prediction confidence and closeness of the 1X2 probabilities;
5. home versus away prediction behaviour;
6. promoted-team and cold-start performance;
7. market disagreement; and
8. other repeatable failure segments revealed by the full 50-match sample.

After GW5, these observations will be converted into explicit, data-driven hypotheses.  Where feasible, **1-3 targeted challenger experiments** will be evaluated using historical same-season-stage evidence, walk-forward testing and untouched out-of-time validation.

A challenger will only replace the champion where the evidence supports promotion.  The objective is not to force more draws or manufacture more varied scorelines.  It is to identify the underlying weakness, test a targeted solution and determine whether it produces a genuine out-of-sample improvement.

This establishes the operating cycle for Football Copilot beyond the initial benchmark period:

**Live performance → insight → hypothesis → targeted experiment → historical and out-of-time validation → champion/challenger decision → next-Gameweek deployment → live measurement.**
